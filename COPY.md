## Story

There’s a little-known waterfall in the Twin Cities, flowing down from the high river bluffs into the Mississippi. It’s called Ivy Falls, and it isn’t found on any maps.

The 25-foot spring-fed waterfall in Mendota Heights was once under consideration to become a state park, but is now all but unknown to most residents of the Twin Cities.

If you’re like me, sometimes you procrastinate by looking at maps. One day while procrastinating, I remembered something Josh Feinberg, a geology professor at the University of Minnesota, told me one spring afternoon in 2020, while we were out collecting water samples from a natural spring on the South banks of the Mississippi, across from campus.

> “So before, there used to be sediment that went all the way across the river,” he said. “And the surface was much higher, there were pre-existing rocks above where the river is now. So as the river eroded down, there are sometimes features that used to be connected with one another in space.”

Because of a geological pattern called jointing, spring-fed waterfalls on the Mississippi in the Twin Cities are often found on opposite sides of the river from each other, flowing down the path of least resistance created by fractures in the layers of rock.

*[First map section: cards over the satellite map, then the elevation map]*

Half-remembering what he told me years later, I bounced back and forth between the banks of the Mississippi on Google Maps, looking at the locations of marked waterfalls and the points directly across. I eventually landed on a spot that looked like it should have a waterfall, but found nothing marked.

A quick look at a topographic map showed there was something there.

*[Plain sheet between the two map sections]*

Reaching Ivy Falls takes about 45 minutes on foot starting at the Lilydale Trailhead, located on the flats of the Mississippi, about a 15-minute drive from Downtown Minneapolis. The town that once resided on this floodplain relocated to the bluffs overlooking the river due to the area’s repeated flooding, [according to the National Parks Service.](https://web.archive.org/web/20240523142348/https://www.nps.gov/miss/planyourvisit/lilydale_park.htm)

*[Route map section: cards over the overview map, which zooms to the close-up]*

The first leg of the journey involves walking two-thirds of a mile along a train track sandwiched between the steep river bluffs and the floodplain forests of Lilydale Regional park. Upon reaching the trailhead, I and a friend assisting in videography, found the road to the parking lot blocked off due to high waters from the recent snow melt of [Minnesota’s third snowiest winter on record.](https://web.archive.org/web/20240523142348/https://www.mprnews.org/story/2023/04/07/seeley-winter-wraps-up-with-a-warm-look-ahead) Traversing a marshy patch of reeds by way of a few conveniently placed wood planks, we reached the railroad tracks, which jutted above the flooded river flats.

The next stretch of the hike saw us walking through the often steep sides of the small wooded valley cut by the stream flowing down from the falls. The trek involves a few crossings of the creek that’s only about 8 feet wide, either by hopping across rocks above the water, or walking across the numerous fallen trees acting as makeshift bridges.

The tiered waterfall has two drops, of about 10 and 25 feet, flowing over shelves of shale before plunging to the stream bed below. The falls are nearly 100% spring-fed, according to Feinberg.

### Happy Hollow Falls

The falls once went under a different name, Happy Hollow Falls. It’s unclear when the name changed.

The falls were little-known among the general public even in the early 20th century. Despite this, the falls and its surrounding area caught the eye of a passionate few. Some of that passion for the “picturesque falls” and “overlooked nook” can be found in this 1917 article from the St. Paul Pioneer Press.

Ivy Falls now sits nestled in between the Mississippi and a residential neighborhood, surrounded by million-dollar house laden cul-de-sacs, according to data from Zillow. A locale once sought as a recreation area for many, now is seen by very few.

## Newspaper transcriptions

These are the original papers’ words, typed up exactly as printed, including their spelling and typos. Highlighted passages are marked ==like this==.

### St. Paul Pioneer Press, Jan. 7, 1917 *[pop-up cards, one per section]*

**St. Paul Beauty Spot That Is Seldom Visited** *[photo caption]*
Here are two views of Happy Hollow falls on the outskirts of St. Paul, ==whose natural beauty is little known to St. Paul people in general.== On the left, the falls after a visit of Jack Frost; the other, view of the upper cataract.

**Picturesque Falls Near City Rivals Minnehaha Itself**
Happy Hollow, West of St. Paul, Well Endowed by Nature.
**In Out of Way Place and Discovered by Few**
Water Drops Into Basin, Then Splashes Over Into Pool Below.

With a pair of skis or snowshoes luring one to the snow-clad hills and silent ravines contributing to the picturesque setting of St. Paul’s natural beauty, one may discover many retreats, modestly concealed from the beaten paths and possessing rare charm of general appeal to the lovers of nature.

**Many Surprises.**
So lavishly has Mother Nature bestowed rugged topographical beauty upon St. Paul and its environs that one need not look beyond the more imposing features of her handicraft to discover that enchantment of hills and lakes and watercourses that lends inspiration to the landscape artist and poet.

And yet, a more critical inspection of the “nooks and corners” will reveal many pleasant surprises, and St. Paul outdoor enthusiasts, invading these previously unfrequented places with skis and toboggans, are finding them out.

**An Overlooked Nook.**
Drop in at the corner drug store or the stationer’s shop and you will find thousands of picture post cards portraying almost every prominent phase of St. Paul’s scenic beauty. Mounds park, of course, with its splendid vista of the Mississippi river below St. Paul; Como, Phalen, the River boulevard, Harriet island, Cherokee heights, Minnehaha falls, and hundreds of other subjects. But you will find no picture of Happy Hollow, with i’s fair rival of the beautiful Minnehaha itself. The postcardmakers have overlooked a bet. It is off the beaten path.

**Is Well Hidden.**
Even the picnickers, whiling away a summer’s afternoon in the cooling shade of the deep glen, may have caught the sound of tinkling, tumbling water without investigating its source. ==One must penetrate the ravine some distance back from Mendota road to discover the falls.== And it is worth the effort.

Far back on the hillsides the sparkling water of a hundred springs mingles in a clear stream that wends its tortuous way among the boulders, tumbling over a series of lesser falls until it reaches the brink of the cataract, where it plunges into the deep valley. In the early spring, when the snow on the hillsides melts under the warm sun, sending a wild torrent of water through the creek bed, the roar of Happy Hollow falls is a fair rival to that of Minnehaha.

**New Longfellow.**
Had Longfellow encountered it at that season he might have written:

> *“And he journeyed without resting,*
> *“Till he heard the cataract’s laughter,*
> *“Heard the falls of Happy Hollow*
> *“Calling to him through the silence.”*

While the silver stream threading its way through Happy Hollow is of lesser volume than Minnehaha creek, the cataract is as deep as Minnehaha, and the gorge as picturesque. Instead of a single, headlong plunge, the water drops over the ledge into a basin, one-third the way to the bottom, and then splashes over into the pool below.

Happy Hollow is spanned by the “Upper Mendota” road bridge, a few miles west of St. Paul. It may be reached by a thirty-minute walk from the end of the Cherokee Heights car line, and its splendid skiing possibilities have attracted many outdoor sports enthusiasts this winter.

### South St. Paul Reporter, Feb. 24, 1937 *[“Read the transcription” toggle]*

**Happy Hollow Park Purchase Is Being Urged**

The first step in the effort to preserve for posterity one of the natural beauty spots of northern Dakota county was taken this week when Representative R. H. Forsythe and Senator Lewis E. Berg ==introduced in the house and senate respectively, bills to set aside what is known as “Happy Hollow Park” as a state park.==

This area, which includes several hundred acres overlooking the Mississippi river near Mendota, is not only outstanding in its scenic advantages but it a spot of historic interest. It marks the sight of the old Indian trail famous in pioneer days and ==because of its location on the river bluff offers the natural opportunities for one of the state’s most beautiful parks.== The site contains a stand of virgin timber and also a little lake.

“There is now an opportunity to buy this area at a moderate cost because the owners reside in a distant state and I think the purchase should be made while there is the chance,” Representative Forsythe said in discussing the proposed park. The bill carries an appropriation of $25,000 and has the backing of numerous organizations interested in conservation and outdoor recreation.

### St. Paul Pioneer Press, Rotogravure, Dec. 27, 1942 *[pop-up over the caption panel]*

**Happy Hollow**
Sentinel trees stand in stark outline against cascading crystal in deserted Happy Hollow. Scene of Summer picnics for every generation of St. Paulites, few persons penetrate its Winter fastness. Late rains brought increased beauty to this recreation spot, turning the trickling stream into an overflowing brook which builds an ever-increasing veil of ice across its rocky ledge. Contrasting with the shimmering exterior of the falls is the weird cavern shown in the picture above, made back of the ice sheet.
